Choosing games for a person living with dementia isn't guesswork — there's decades of research showing what engages the brain without causing distress. The best games combine familiar formats with evidence-informed design principles that protect dignity while stimulating neural pathways.

## The science of engagement in dementia

### Errorless Learning (Baddeley & Wilson, 1994)

Traditional games teach through trial and error — you make a mistake, you learn from it. But for people with dementia, errors don't lead to learning. They lead to confusion, frustration, and a reinforced sense of failure.

Errorless learning flips this: show the answer first, guide the person to success, never let them sit in a failure state. Games designed on this principle ensure the person always completes the activity — building confidence rather than eroding it.

**What this looks like in practice:** A matching game that briefly shows all cards face-up before flipping them. A Bingo game where hints gently highlight the correct square if the person hesitates. A word puzzle that reveals letters progressively rather than punishing wrong guesses.

### 40Hz Gamma Entrainment (Iaccarino et al., Nature, 2016)

![Sound wave visualization](/assets/images/articles//sound-waves.jpg)


Groundbreaking research from MIT demonstrated that exposing mice with Alzheimer's pathology to 40Hz sensory stimulation (light and sound) reduced amyloid plaques and tau tangles — the hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease. Subsequent human studies (Chan et al., 2022; He et al., Cell, 2023) showed that 40Hz auditory and visual stimulation is safe, well-tolerated, and associated with improved neural oscillatory activity in people with mild cognitive impairment.

**What this means for games:** An app that plays a gentle 40Hz background tone (mixed with calming ambient sounds like ocean waves or birdsong) during gameplay provides potential neuroprotective stimulation without requiring any effort from the person. They simply play their game while the tone runs in the background.

The key is that 40Hz is below conscious perception for most people — they hear a gentle hum, not an annoying buzz. When mixed with nature sounds, it becomes a pleasant ambient backdrop.

### 4Hz Theta Rhythm and Relaxation

Theta brainwaves (4-8Hz) are associated with deep relaxation, meditation, and the transition between wakefulness and sleep. A subtle 4Hz visual rhythm — a gentle, slow pulsing of screen brightness — can promote calm without requiring the person to "do" anything.

Research on rhythmic sensory stimulation shows that the brain naturally synchronizes with external rhythms (a phenomenon called "entrainment"). A 4Hz visual pulse encourages theta-band activity, which is associated with reduced anxiety and improved emotional regulation.

**Important:** This should always be optional and caregiver-controlled. Some people with dementia are sensitive to visual changes — particularly those with Lewy Body Dementia who may experience visual hallucinations. The ability to turn this off per person is essential.

### Musical Memory Preservation

![Older person enjoying music](/assets/images/articles//elderly-headphones.jpg)


Musical memory is stored in the supplementary motor area and cerebellum — brain regions that are relatively preserved even in advanced Alzheimer's disease (Jacobsen et al., Brain, 2015). This is why a person who can't remember their children's names may still sing along perfectly to a song from 1960.

Games that use familiar melodies, rhythm, and musical patterns tap into this preserved pathway. Bell tones, simple melodies, and rhythmic tapping exercises can reach people that visual or language-based games cannot.

## What the best games have in common

Based on the research, the ideal dementia game:

1. **Uses errorless learning** — guides to success, never punishes failure
2. **Provides multisensory feedback** — sound on every touch, visual confirmation, physical response
3. **Adapts invisibly** — difficulty adjusts without the person knowing
4. **Offers optional 40Hz stimulation** — background tone for potential neuroprotective benefit
5. **Includes musical/rhythmic elements** — taps into preserved memory pathways
6. **Allows calming transitions** — when engagement drops, transitions to relaxation rather than a frozen screen
7. **Respects dignity** — no childish graphics, no visible scores, no "Game Over"

## Games that meet these criteria

**Bingo** — Familiar format stored in procedural memory. The word "BINGO!" triggers decades of positive associations. Combined with 40Hz background audio, it provides both engagement and potential stimulation.

**Rhythm games** — Tapping to a beat exercises the cerebellum and motor cortex. When the beat is generated at frequencies related to gamma entrainment, the rhythmic interaction itself becomes a form of stimulation.

**Melody recognition** — "Name That Tune" style games access the preserved musical memory pathway. Even people in later stages may recognize a melody they can't name.

**Visual matching** — Simple, concrete, no reading required. When combined with a subtle 4Hz visual rhythm in the background, the person receives theta-band relaxation while actively engaging their visual cortex.
