You bought the tablet hoping it would help. Maybe you imagined them watching videos, playing simple games, or video-calling grandchildren. But the reality is different — they tap the wrong thing, get confused by pop-ups, accidentally close the app, and hand it back to you frustrated.

Here's how to make tablet time actually work.

## Set up the environment first

**One app, full screen.** Don't hand them a tablet with 30 app icons. Open the one app you want them to use and nothing else. On iPad, use Guided Access (Settings → Accessibility → Guided Access) to lock them into a single app. On Android, use Screen Pinning.

**Brightness up, volume up.** Age-related vision and hearing loss means they need more of both than you'd expect. Turn brightness to 80%+ and volume to at least 60%.

**Stable surface.** Don't hand them a tablet to hold — prop it on a table or use a case with a stand. Holding a tablet requires grip strength and spatial awareness that may be impaired.

**Stay nearby for the first session.** Not hovering — just present. "I'll be right here if you need me" gives them permission to try without fear.

## Choose the right content

![Spark Mind Play category selection](/assets/images/articles//game-list-2x2.png)


**Games beat videos.** Passive watching (YouTube, TV) doesn't engage the brain the same way active participation does. A game that requires them to tap, choose, or respond keeps neural pathways active.

**Familiar beats novel.** A digital Bingo game is better than a novel puzzle they've never seen. Familiarity reduces the cognitive load of "what am I supposed to do?" and lets them focus on doing it.

**Short sessions.** 10-20 minutes is ideal. Cognitive fatigue sets in faster than you'd expect. An app that recognizes fatigue and offers a break is better than one that keeps going until they're agitated.

**No reading required.** If the game requires reading instructions, it won't work for moderate-to-late stages. Look for games that teach through demonstration (showing the answer briefly before starting) rather than text.

## What success looks like

Don't measure success by "did they win?" Measure it by:
- Did they stay engaged for more than 5 minutes?
- Did they seem calm during play?
- Did they tap independently (not waiting for you to tell them what to do)?
- Did they smile, laugh, or show recognition?

If any of these happened, the session was successful — regardless of whether they "completed" the game.

## When it's not working

**They push the tablet away** — the game is too hard, too confusing, or too visually cluttered. Try a simpler game or a different app.

**They tap randomly without looking** — they don't understand the cause-and-effect. Try a game with stronger audio/visual feedback on every tap.

**They get agitated** — stop immediately. No game is worth distress. Try again tomorrow with something different.

**They fall asleep** — that's okay. It might mean the session was calming. Or it might mean they're fatigued. Either way, let them rest.
