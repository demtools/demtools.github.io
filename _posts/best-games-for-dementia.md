# What Are the Best Games for Someone with Dementia?


![Older person enjoying a tablet game](/assets/images/articles//hero-elderly-tablet.jpg)

Finding the right game for a person living with dementia isn't about difficulty — it's about dignity. The best games share a few key traits: they're familiar, they don't punish mistakes, and they adapt to the person's ability without making them feel tested.

## What to look for

**No failure states.** If a game shows "Game Over," flashes red, or makes a buzzer sound, it's not designed for this population. Those signals cause confusion, anxiety, and agitation. The person doesn't understand why the screen is angry — they just know something went wrong.

**Familiar formats.** Bingo, word search, card matching, simple puzzles — these are activities stored in long-term procedural memory. A person who can't remember what they had for breakfast may still know how to play Bingo. That familiarity is therapeutic.

**Large, clear visuals.** Small text, cluttered screens, and abstract icons create cognitive overload. Look for games with large touch targets (at least 64 pixels), high contrast, and minimal on-screen elements.

**Adaptive difficulty.** A game that's too easy is boring. Too hard is frustrating. The best games adjust automatically based on how the person is doing — without requiring the caregiver to change settings.

**Sound on every interaction.** Silence is disorienting for people with dementia. Every tap should produce a gentle sound that confirms "something happened." This provides the sensory feedback their brain needs to connect action with result.

## Games that work well

![Screenshot of Spark Mind Play Bingo game](/assets/images/articles//bingo-win.png)


**Bingo** — The most universally recognized game for older adults. The word "BINGO!" itself can trigger joy from decades of community hall memories. Look for digital versions that call out items visually (not just numbers) and celebrate the win enthusiastically.

**Picture matching** — Show a target image, find the match from options. Simple, visual, no reading required. Works across all stages of dementia.

**Tap-along rhythm games** — Tapping to a beat exercises motor coordination and provides satisfying audio feedback. The key is a forgiving timing window — if the beat detection is too strict, it becomes frustrating.

**Word search** — For people in earlier stages who can still read, finding hidden words in a grid is familiar (like the Sunday newspaper) and provides a sense of accomplishment.

**Sorting games** — Putting items into categories (food vs. not food, winter vs. summer) exercises executive function in a concrete, visual way.

## What to avoid

- Games designed for children (patronizing graphics, baby voices)
- Timed challenges (creates pressure and anxiety)
- Games requiring multi-step memory (remember this, now go to another screen, now enter it)
- Anything with in-app purchases or complex menus
- Games that require reading instructions to play
