# How to Keep a Person with Alzheimer's Engaged on a Tablet: What Neuroscience Tells Us


![Brain with highlighted regions](/assets/images/articles//brain-game-regions.png)

Keeping someone with Alzheimer's engaged isn't about finding the right app — it's about understanding how their brain still works and designing the experience around those preserved pathways.

## The neuroscience of engagement in Alzheimer's

### What's impaired

- **Episodic memory** (hippocampus) — they can't form new memories of what just happened
- **Executive function** (prefrontal cortex) — they can't plan, sequence, or hold multiple rules in mind
- **Working memory** — they can't remember something from one screen to use on the next
- **Language processing** (in later stages) — written instructions become meaningless

### What's preserved (often until late stages)

- **Procedural memory** (basal ganglia) — how to do familiar tasks (play cards, tap a rhythm, sort objects)
- **Musical memory** (supplementary motor area, cerebellum) — melodies, rhythms, songs from their era
- **Emotional memory** (amygdala) — they feel joy, recognition, comfort even when they can't explain why
- **Sensory processing** — they still respond to touch, sound, colour, movement
- **Motor patterns** — tapping, pointing, pressing buttons

### The engagement equation

Engagement happens when you activate preserved pathways while avoiding impaired ones. A game that requires episodic memory (remember where the card was) will frustrate. A game that uses procedural memory (tap the matching picture) will succeed.

## How sensory stimulation supports engagement

![40Hz audio visualization](/assets/images/articles//sound-waves.jpg)


### 40Hz Auditory Stimulation

Research published in Nature (Iaccarino et al., 2016) and Cell (He et al., 2023) demonstrated that 40Hz sensory stimulation promotes gamma brainwave oscillations — the neural frequency associated with attention, perception, and memory consolidation.

In practical terms: playing a gentle 40Hz tone in the background during tablet activities may help the brain maintain the attentional state needed for engagement. The person doesn't need to "do" anything with the tone — it works passively while they play.

**How to use it:** Look for apps that offer optional 40Hz background audio. It should be mixed with calming sounds (not a raw tone) and controllable by the caregiver. Start with low volume and observe — if the person seems more focused or calm, keep it. If they seem agitated, turn it off.

### 4Hz Visual Rhythm

Theta-band (4-8Hz) visual stimulation promotes relaxation and reduces anxiety. A very subtle, slow pulsing of screen brightness (barely perceptible) can help the person stay calm during gameplay.

This is particularly useful for people who become anxious when faced with a task. The gentle rhythm provides a subconscious "it's okay, relax" signal while they engage with the game content.

**Caution:** Always optional. People with Lewy Body Dementia or photosensitive epilepsy should not use visual rhythm features. A good app lets you disable this per person.

### Varied Ambient Soundscapes

The brain habituates to repetitive stimuli — if the same ocean waves play every session, the brain stops registering them within minutes. Varied ambient sounds (ocean one day, river the next, birdsong the next) maintain the calming effect because the brain treats each as novel.

Research on "soft fascination" (Kaplan, 1995) shows that natural sounds reduce cognitive fatigue and restore attention. Playing nature sounds during tablet activities creates an environment where the brain can engage without depleting its limited resources.

## Practical strategies backed by research

### 1. Use the "errorless" approach

Don't let them fail. Research (Clare & Jones, 2008) shows that people with Alzheimer's learn better when errors are prevented rather than corrected. Choose apps that guide to success rather than testing and scoring.

### 2. Keep sessions short (10-20 minutes)

Cognitive fatigue in Alzheimer's is real and measurable. After 15-20 minutes of active engagement, neural resources are depleted. An app that detects fatigue (slowing responses, increased errors, disengagement) and offers a break is better than one that keeps going until agitation.

### 3. Activate musical memory

If the person isn't responding to visual games, try music. Play familiar melodies and ask them to tap along, or use a "Name That Tune" format. Musical memory is stored separately from other types and is often the last to fade.

### 4. Provide physical feedback on every touch

The somatosensory cortex (touch processing) remains functional in most stages of dementia. An app that provides a physical "press" response (the button visually pushes in when touched) creates a stronger brain-body connection than a flat screen that just changes colour.

### 5. Use the 40Hz background (if tolerated)

Start with it off. After a few sessions when the person is comfortable with the games, enable the 40Hz background audio at low volume. Observe over 2-3 sessions. If engagement time increases or agitation decreases, it's helping.
